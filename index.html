ハコスカ

Hakosuka. Nissan Skyline 2000GT-R, 1969 to 1972.

Six details that made it

  1. 1
    S20 straight-sixA 1,989 cc twin-cam with four valves per cylinder, reworked for the road from the engine in Prince's R380 prototype racer. 160 PS at 7,000 rpm.
  2. 2
    The box noseA flat, upright front with four round lamps. Hako means box, and suka is short for Skyline (sukairain). The nickname came from fans, not the factory.
  3. 3
    Pillarless hardtopThe 1970 two-door has no post between the door glass and the rear side window, so the whole side opens up.
  4. 4
    Surf lineThe crease that runs along the flank and rolls over the rear wheel. It is the styling signature of this Skyline generation.
  5. 5
    Rear overfendersThe hardtop wears bolt-on arch extensions to cover wider rear tyres. The surf line is cut away to make room for them.
  6. 6
    100 litre fuel tankA tank sized for long races, in a road car. Inside it is just as single-minded: fixed bucket seats and almost no comforts.
The story

A racing engine in a family sedan

The GT-R starts with Prince, a smaller carmaker that had been chasing Porsches with stretched Skyline sedans since the 1964 Japan Grand Prix. Prince went on to build a pure racing prototype, the R380, which won that race in 1966. The same year, Prince merged into Nissan and its engineers came with it.

In February 1969 those engineers, led by Shinichiro Sakurai, put a road version of the R380's engine into the plain four-door C10 Skyline. The result was the Skyline 2000GT-R, chassis code PGC10. It looked like a salaryman's sedan and revved like a race car.

In 1970 the GT-R moved to the new two-door hardtop body, the KPGC10. Its wheelbase is 70 mm shorter than the sedan's, which made it turn in more sharply. This is the shape most people picture when they hear Hakosuka.

The line ended in 1972. Its successor, the Kenmeri GT-R, lasted only a few months in 1973, with just 197 built. The GT-R badge then went quiet until the R32 arrived in 1989.

  2. 2

The story

The GT-R that never raced

The C110 Skyline arrived in 1972 with one of Japan's best-known advertising campaigns. It followed a young couple, Ken and Mary, on road trips through the countryside. People began calling the car Ken to Mary no Skyline, shortened to Kenmeri.

A racing version was shown at the 1972 Tokyo Motor Show, and fans expected it to carry on the Hakosuka's winning run. The road-going GT-R reached dealers in January 1973.

It was gone by April. Tighter emissions rules and the oil crisis ended the S20 engine, and with it the GT-R. Only 197 cars were built, and the race car never turned a wheel in competition.

That makes the Kenmeri the rarest GT-R of all. The badge did not return for 16 years.

  5. 5
    Rear wingA fixed wing on the boot lid, shaped in the wind tunnel for the race car.
  6. 6
    Round tail lampsFour round lamps, brought back from the Kenmeri of 1973.
The story

Built to win Group A

By the late 1980s Nissan wanted a touring car that could beat the Ford Sierra Cosworth. Its engineers started from the Group A rulebook and worked backwards: a 2.6 litre twin-turbo engine, four-wheel drive and the widest tyres the class allowed.

The road car went on sale in August 1989, three months after the regular R32 Skyline, and brought the GT-R name back after 16 years.

It began racing in March 1990 and never lost a race in the All Japan Touring Car Championship, winning all 29 it started through 1993. In Australia it won the Bathurst 1000 in 1991 and 1992.

The Australian magazine Wheels nicknamed it Godzilla in 1989, the monster from Japan. The name has followed every GT-R since.

The story

Faster, and criticised for it

The R33 GT-R was shown as a prototype at the 1993 Tokyo Motor Show and went on sale in January 1995. It was longer, wider and about 100 kg heavier than the R32, and many fans said it had gone soft.

The stopwatch disagreed. A factory test car lapped the Nürburgring 21 seconds quicker than the R32, and Nissan advertised it as the first production car under eight minutes there.

With Group A racing over, NISMO took a rear-wheel-drive version to the 24 Hours of Le Mans in 1995 and 1996. The best result was 10th overall in 1995.

Today the R33 is valued for the things it was once criticised for. It is the calmest GT-R at speed and the easiest to live with.

The story

Smaller, sharper, last

The R34 GT-R made its debut in January 1999. Nissan listened to the complaints about the R33 and made the new car shorter and stiffer, with a blunt, squared-off body.

The engine was the familiar RB26DETT, still rated at 280 PS under the Japanese makers' voluntary limit. A six-speed gearbox was new, and so was the display that showed the driver what the engine was doing.

On track, the Pennzoil NISMO GT-R took Érik Comas to the 1999 Japan GT Championship title.

Production ended in August 2002 after about 11,600 cars. The final run included two Nür editions, named after the Nürburgring and limited to 1,000 each. The next GT-R, in 2007, dropped the Skyline name.

The story

A sports car people could afford

The Fairlady Z went on sale in Japan in November 1969, replacing an open roadster with a closed coupe. At home it began with two-litre engines. For export, mainly to North America, it was given the 2.4 litre L24 and named the Datsun 240Z.

A team led by Yoshihiko Matsuo drew the shape. It offered the looks of a European GT at a far lower price, and it started every morning.

The car proved itself the hard way. It won the East African Safari Rally outright in 1971 and 1973 and finished third on the 1972 Monte Carlo Rally.

Japan got its own 240Z in November 1971. The rarest early Z is the Z432, which used the S20 engine from the Hakosuka GT-R.

The story

The Z returns

Production of the previous Z, the 300ZX, ended in August 2000 and for two years Nissan sold no Z at all. Inside the company and among owners there was steady pressure to bring it back.

The Z33 went on sale in July 2002 in Japan and the United States. It went back to the first car's formula: two seats, a big six-cylinder engine with no turbo, rear-wheel drive and a price that ordinary buyers could reach.

The engine was a 3.5 litre V6, which gave the export car its name, 350Z.

It ran until December 2008 and sold more than 250,000, far more than the 300ZX before it.

The story

The same idea, tightened

The Z34 went on sale in Japan on 1 December 2008. Nissan kept the 350Z's layout and changed nearly every part: a shorter wheelbase, a wider body and a bigger 3.7 litre engine.

Its party trick was SynchroRev Match. Change down with the manual gearbox and the car blips the throttle for you, as a skilled driver would with heel and toe.

A Roadster and a 40th anniversary edition followed in 2009.

The 370Z stayed in production for twelve years with only light updates. The 2020 model year was its last, and the current Z is built on a modified version of the same platform.

The story

Grown-up, then sharpened

The S13 Silvia had been a huge hit, and in 1993 Nissan replaced it with a bigger, smoother car. The S14 was wider and heavier, and buyers were cool on it at first.

Built because the rules said so

To enter the World Rally Championship in Group A, a maker had to sell road-going versions of its rally car. In 1992 Mitsubishi took the engine and four-wheel drive from the larger Galant VR-4 and fitted them into the small, light Lancer. That was the Evolution I.

The story

The sports car built around a rotor

Mazda bet its engineering name on the Wankel rotary engine. Instead of pistons going up and down, a triangular rotor spins inside an oval housing. The result is a very compact, light engine that loves to rev.

The first RX-7, the SA22C, arrived in March 1978 with the engine mounted behind the front axle and pop-up headlamps. It was the best seller of the three generations, with more than 470,000 built. The FC followed in 1985.

The FD of 1991 is the one in the drawing. Mazda put it through a weight-cutting programme and gave it the 13B-REW engine with two turbochargers working in sequence. Power rose from 255 PS at launch to 280 PS in later cars.

Production ended in August 2002 with the Spirit R, a final run of about 1,500 cars. Across 24 years Mazda built 811,634 RX-7s.

The story

A grand tourer that tuners made a legend

The Supra began in 1978 as a longer, six-cylinder version of the Celica. By its fourth generation it was Toyota's flagship sports car, and it was unveiled at the 1993 Chicago Motor Show. Japanese sales started in May 1993.

Toyota worked to keep weight down and gave the top model the 2JZ-GTE engine. Japanese cars were rated at 280 PS under the makers' voluntary limit, and export cars were quoted higher.

The engine turned out to be far stronger than it needed to be. Tuners found it could take big power increases on standard internals, and the Supra became the car to build for drag strips and top-speed runs.

US sales ended in 1998. Production continued in Japan until August 2002, when tighter emissions rules closed it out. Toyota did not use the Supra name again until 2019.

Named after a legend, built with a rival

Toyota wanted a small, light, affordable sports car again. Chief engineer Tetsuya Tada led the project and brought in Subaru, which supplied its flat-four engine layout and built the car at its Gunma plant. Subaru sold its own version as the BRZ.

It was sold as the 86 in Japan and Australasia, the GT86 in Europe and the Scion FR-S in North America. An update in 2016 brought new styling and a stiffer body, and the line continues today as the GR86.
